Ask a 10-K a question

This searches real SEC filings with the retrieval pipeline from finance-rag-eval. It reads the company and period from your question, narrows the search to those filings, ranks their pages with BM25, and puts balance sheet, income statement and cash flow pages first when the question needs one. All of it runs in your browser.

  • (one the pipeline gets wrong)

Pick an example or type a question. The first search fetches 114 KB of index data, then about 170 KB of compressed page text for each filing it opens.

    The demo holds the 84 filings that FinanceBench questions cite, from 32 companies, out of the benchmark's 360. Checked against the Python pipeline on every benchmark question it can search (146 of 150, 438 question and setting pairs): same top 10 pages in the same order, same scores to the last bit. The two switches turn off the last two steps in the table below, so you can see what each one does to a single question.

    What each step is worth

    FinanceBench has 150 analyst questions over 360 filings (53,399 pages), and each question is labeled with the page that answers it. That makes retrieval measurable without grading generated answers: did the right page make the top 10? I split the questions once into 50 for development and 100 held out. Everything I tuned, I tuned on the 50; the table is the 100.

    Filings share most of their vocabulary, so BM25 over everything puts a page from the right filing in its top 10 only 42% of the time. The filter reads the company, year and quarter from the question. It recognized the company in 97 of the 100 held-out questions, kept the filing with the answer for 96, narrowed 360 filings to 2.5 on average, and took Hit@10 from 0.14 to 0.43 (+0.29, 95% interval +0.20 to +0.38).

    Routing is worth as much again, and it's a small piece of domain knowledge. For 78 of the 150 questions the labeled page is a balance sheet, income statement or cash flow statement: a table of numbers that shares almost no words with "what is the FY2019 fixed asset turnover ratio". Questions that name a statement, or a line item or ratio that lives on one, get those pages first. 62 of the 100 held-out questions trigger it, and Hit@10 went from 0.43 to 0.71 (+0.28, 95% interval +0.19 to +0.37).

    Leaving the company's name out of the query is the step I'm least sure of. Without routing it hurts (0.43 to 0.33), because the name is one of the few words a statement page shares with the question. With routing it adds +0.04, 95% interval -0.02 to +0.10, which is within noise.

    The embedding model didn't earn its cost. Fused with BM25, MiniLM scored 0.73 against BM25's 0.75 on the held-out questions (-0.02, 95% interval -0.08 to +0.03) and takes 13 ms a query instead of 2.4. On the 50 development questions it looked better (0.78 against 0.66), which is why it's the configuration I committed to before testing; a gap that size on 50 questions turned out to be noise. Without the filter MiniLM does beat BM25 (0.24 against 0.14), because it finds the right filing more often (61% against 42%). The filter makes that redundant.

    25 of the held-out questions need more than one page. The BM25 pipeline gets 66% of their labeled pages into the top 10, and the hybrid 64%.

    Where it still misses

    Each held-out question whose answer page didn't make the top 10 goes in the first stage of the pipeline that lost it. The rules run on every evaluation, so the counts change when the code does. The biggest group, narrative pages that share almost no words with the question, is the case a cross-encoder reranker is meant for.

    As a service

    Behind the FastAPI service, with one worker pinned to one CPU core and the load generator on the other, BM25 serves 126 requests a second to a single client (p50 7.6 ms, p95 12 ms) and up to 162 as clients are added; the hybrid tops out at 55. No request failed at any load. Pricing one vCPU at half an AWS c7i.large's on-demand rate, that's $0.10 per million BM25 queries and $0.25 for the hybrid, if this throughput carries over to that vCPU, which I haven't measured.

    Two smaller checks

    The BM25 index is kept in segments so filings can be added without a rebuild. With 36 filings held out and added back in batches, every score on 300 queries matched a one-pass build exactly, and so did every top 100. A full build takes 13.1 s; each batch took 0.42 to 0.64 s.

    Chunking pages into smaller passages didn't help. 650-token chunks score the same as whole pages (0.75), and 256- and 128-token chunks score 0.71 and 0.72 while indexing two to four times as many units, so the index stays at one entry per page.

    Who to call after discharge

    At Cigna I judged readmission models by how many high-need patients a fixed outreach capacity would reach, alongside AUC. readmission-capacity is the same problem on public data: 101,766 diabetic inpatient encounters from 130 US hospitals (UCI, 1999 to 2008), split by patient, with death and hospice discharges removed. On 19,745 held-out encounters, a LightGBM call list the size of 10% of discharges holds 25.0% of the patients who were back within 30 days, against 20.5% when calling whoever had the most inpatient stays in the prior year and 15.8% with the LACE index.

    Score a patient

    The trees run in your browser and give LightGBM's own scores, which the tests check on 300 held-out rows here and 2,000 in the project's CI. Inputs not on the form are held at the held-out set's median or most common value.

    The boosting library doesn't matter here: XGBoost's list holds 24.8% of the readmissions to LightGBM's 25.0%, and paired over the same patients LightGBM is ahead by 0.2 points (95% interval -0.8 to +1.1). LightGBM ships because it led on validation. Against the prior-stays rule it reaches 4.4 points more of the readmissions (+2.7 to +6.0).

    The calibrated probabilities said the 10% list would hold 539 readmissions, and it held 569. The list has a blind spot: 943 of the 2,279 readmissions (41%) were patients with no inpatient, emergency or outpatient visit in the year before, and the list holds 110 of them (12%). They're 833 of its 1,710 misses.

    The extract has no admission dates, so drift over time can't be tested with it. Instead I shift the held-out set on purpose. With patients 70 and over made 70% of discharges, the list holds 23.9% of readmissions, the model expects 523 on it where 585 happen, and the PSI check on age fires. Blanking the second and third diagnoses on 30% of encounters barely moves the list (25.1%), no PSI alert fires, and only the missing-value check notices.

    The scoring API, on one CPU core with the load generator on another, answers 72 single-encounter requests a second (p50 13.3 ms, p95 18.7 ms), and scores about 515 encounters a second in batches of 100, reasons included. No request failed.

    Code, the evaluation, the service and its load test: github.com/bharathkumardev1/readmission-capacity. Numbers from commit 9ea167a, 2026-10-21.

    A research assistant that reviews its own answers

    Agentic Research Assistant answers questions about research papers and cites the passages it used. A second model call reviews each answer against the passages it cites and, when something is missing, writes a new search query; the loop runs up to three rounds and stops early at a deadline, at a spending cap, or when a search finds nothing new. What I'm measuring is whether the review loop earns its extra calls over a single pass: on QASPER's questions about NLP papers, with the dataset's official answer and evidence F1, a model judge I check against my own grades, and what each system costs in dollars and seconds. Every model reply is recorded, so CI reruns the evaluation without an API key.
