Finding the page that answers a question about a 10-K

An answer about a filing is only as good as the pages retrieval puts in front of the model. FinanceBench labels the answer page for every question, so I measured retrieval on its own, one step at a time, on questions I never tuned on.

Benchmark
FinanceBench: 150 analyst questions over 360 SEC filings, 53,399 pages
Held out
100 questions kept out of all tuning, which used the other 50
Result
Hit@10 from 0.14 to 0.75 on the held-out questions
Serving
BM25 on one core: 126 queries a second at 12 ms p95

It covers the 84 filings that FinanceBench questions cite. On all 438 question and setting pairs the tests run, it returns the same top 10 pages as the Python pipeline, with the same scores to the last bit.

How it narrows the search

Every 10-K has a balance sheet, a risk section and the same accounting terms, so BM25 over all 53,399 pages finds the right filing for only 42% of held-out questions. The first step isn't ranking: the filter reads the company, fiscal year and quarter from the question and keeps 2.5 of 360 filings on average. Questions about a financial statement then get that statement's pages first.

What each step is worth

I tuned on 50 questions and kept 100 aside; everything here is the 100. Hit@10 asks whether the labeled page made the top 10, which is what a model answering from ten pages needs.

The filter found the company in 97 of 100 questions and kept the right filing for 96, taking Hit@10 from 0.14 to 0.43 (+0.29, 95% interval +0.20 to +0.38). Routing added as much again. 78 of the 150 answer pages are financial statements, tables of numbers that share almost no words with a question like "what is the FY2019 fixed asset turnover ratio". Routing fires on 62 held-out questions and took Hit@10 to 0.71 (+0.28, +0.19 to +0.37).

Leaving the company's name out of the query hurts without routing (0.43 to 0.33) and adds +0.04 with it, inside noise (-0.02 to +0.10). It's the step I'm least sure of.

MiniLM didn't earn its cost. Fused with BM25 it scored 0.73 against 0.75 (-0.02, -0.08 to +0.03) at 13 ms a query instead of 2.4. It looked better on the 50 dev questions (0.78 against 0.66), which is why I committed to it before testing; that gap was noise. Without the filter MiniLM does win (0.24 against 0.14) by finding the right filing more often (61% against 42%), and the filter makes that redundant.

Where it still misses

Each held-out question the hybrid pipeline missed is filed under the first stage that lost it. The rules run on every evaluation, so the counts follow the code.

The biggest group, narrative pages that share almost no words with the question, is the case a cross-encoder reranker is meant for. I tried one, and it made retrieval worse (below). Routing's own failures are few: 2 questions needed a statement page without saying so, and 1 routed to the right kind of statement but ranked a different statement page first.

As a service

With one FastAPI worker pinned to one core and the load generator on the other, BM25 serves 126 requests a second to one client (p95 12 ms) and up to 162 with more clients; the hybrid tops out at 55. No request failed.

At half an AWS c7i.large's on-demand price, one vCPU, that's $0.10 per million BM25 queries and $0.25 for the hybrid, if the throughput carries over to that machine, which I haven't measured.

Two more checks

Adding filings without a rebuild. The index is kept in segments. With 36 filings held out and added back in batches, every score on 300 queries matched a one-pass build exactly. A full build takes 13.1 s; a batch took 0.42 to 0.64 s.

Chunk size. Smaller passages didn't help: 650-token chunks tie whole pages (0.75), and 256- and 128-token chunks score 0.71 and 0.72 with two to four times as many entries.

Does a reranker help?

A cross-encoder reads the question and a page together, which is the usual fix for the narrative-page misses above. I ran BAAI/bge-reranker-base over the top 30 pages of three retrieval systems and re-sorted them by its score. It made the two systems that work worse and left the all-pages baseline within one question of where it was.

On the held-out questions the full pipeline's Hit@10 fell from 0.73 to 0.63 (-0.10, 95% interval -0.20 to -0.01; 7 questions better, 17 worse) and its MRR@10 from 0.542 to 0.336. The 50 dev questions agree: 0.78 to 0.62.

The model reads 512 tokens, so a long page is scored on its first part. I reran the full pipeline with up to three windows per page, scored as the best window. Hit@10 was 0.57, lower than one window (0.63) and -0.16, 95% interval -0.26 to -0.06 against no reranker, so truncation doesn't explain it.

Scoring took 367 ms a page with one window and 909 ms with three on a laptop CPU, so re-sorting 30 pages would add about 11 s to a query. The reported system stays without a reranker. This is one off-the-shelf model, not fine-tuned and not tuned on this data; I haven't tried others.

What the model does with the pages

Retrieval is a means. To see what it buys, gpt-6-luna answered each held-out question from four page sets, and a stronger model, gpt-6.1-sol, graded every answer against FinanceBench's reference. An answer of "not in these pages" counts as incorrect.

With the gold pages it got 83 of 100 right. With the pipeline's top 5 it got 66, a gap of 0.17 (95% interval 0.09 to 0.25; 18 questions better with gold pages, 1 worse). The loss sits in the questions where retrieval missed: when a top-5 page was a gold page (66 questions) it scored 0.83, and when none was (34) 0.32.

The pages are worth +0.29 over no pages at all (95% interval +0.16 to +0.41), not the whole 0.66, because the model already knows some figures for well-known companies and got 37 of 100 with none. Searching all pages with BM25 was worse than no pages: the model declined 79 of 100 questions.

A model grading a model needs a check, so I graded 60 of the judge's verdicts myself, from 51 questions, against the same rubric and FinanceBench's references. I used AI tools to find the filings and check figures, so this grade is research-assisted, not blind to AI input. The judge agreed on 59 of 60 (kappa 0.95, 95% interval 0.83 to 1.00). The one difference was a Corning working-capital answer I marked correct and the judge marked incorrect.

The labels follow the references, which are debatable in places: one says Pfizer was still spinning off Upjohn in 2023, when it had finished in 2020, and another gives a JPMorgan per-share figure that is tangible book value. So accuracy here means agreement with FinanceBench. It's one run with one generator and one grader, and it cost $0.46.

Run it yourself

FinanceBench is pinned to a commit and every PDF is checked against its SHA-256, so a rerun scores the same pages.

make install
make data      # 150 questions, 360 PDFs (about 670 MB), page text
make embed     # MiniLM over every page: hours on a laptop CPU, resumable
make eval      # every configuration on dev and test, and RESULTS.md
make studies   # the incremental-index check and the chunk-size study
make loadtest

CI runs the tests on Python 3.11 and 3.13, a regression gate that fails if Hit@10 on the dev questions drops by more than one question, a smoke test of the Docker image, a secret scan, and the browser port's parity test.
